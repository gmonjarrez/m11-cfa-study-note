1 Introduction

Using UCLA’s SAQ-8 data, this note compares one-factor, two-factor, and second-order CFA models.

2 Data and software

3 CFA concepts

3.1 Measurement model and implied covariance

An observed indicator is a recorded item response. A latent factor is a construct inferred from common variation in the indicators. For item ii, the measurement equation is yi=τi+λiη+ϵiy_i = \tau_i + \lambda_i\eta + \epsilon_i, where τi\tau_i is an intercept, η\eta is the latent factor, λi\lambda_i is the loading, and ϵi\epsilon_i is the residual. The covariance-only models below use the centered form of this equation.

If residuals are uncorrelated with the factors, the implied item covariance matrix is Σ̂=Λ̂Φ̂Λ̂⊤+Θ̂\widehat\Sigma = \widehat\Lambda\widehat\Phi\widehat\Lambda^\top + \widehat\Theta. The loading matrix Λ\Lambda links factors to items, Φ\Phi gives the covariance of the factors, and Θ\Theta contains the item residual variances and any modeled residual covariances. Here Θ\Theta is diagonal. For example, the three-item model predicts Cov⁡(q03,q04)=λ03λ04Var⁡(f)\operatorname{Cov}(q03,q04) = \lambda_{03}\lambda_{04}\operatorname{Var}(f).

In a path diagram, ovals represent factors, rectangles represent items, single-headed arrows show loadings, and double-headed arrows show covariances.

3.2 Identification and degrees of freedom

Identification asks whether the observed moments determine a unique parameter solution once the scale and direction of each factor are set. The known values are distinct observed variances and covariances. Free parameters are estimated, and fixed parameters are assigned values by the model. For pp items and a covariance-only model, df=p(p+1)/2−kdf = p(p+1)/2 - k, where kk is the number of independent free parameters.

Example Known moments Free parameters Degrees of freedom
Three items with marker loading 6 2 loadings + 3 residual variances + 1 factor variance 0
Three items with unit factor variance 6 3 loadings + 3 residual variances 0
Two items with only a marker constraint 3 1 loading + 2 residual variances + 1 factor variance −1
Eight items with marker loading 36 7 loadings + 8 residual variances + 1 factor variance 20

The two-item model is under-identified. Both three-item models are just-identified, and the eight-item model is over-identified. A nonnegative degrees-of-freedom count is necessary for identification, and the model structure must also permit unique estimates.

4 One-factor CFA

4.1 Three-item model

The operator =~ assigns q03, q04, and q05 to factor f. The marker method fixes the q03 loading to 1 and estimates the factor variance. The unit-variance method frees the q03 loading with NA* and fixes the variance to 1.

m1a <- 'f =~ q03 + q04 + q05'
m1b <- '
  f =~ NA*q03 + q04 + q05
  f ~~ 1*f
'
marker <- cfa(m1a, data = saq)
unit <- cfa(m1b, data = saq)

5 Two-factor CFA

UCLA omits q02. SPSS Anxiety uses q01, q03–q05, and q08. Attribution Bias uses the two computer items, q06 and q07.

loading_table <- function(fit) {
  estimates <- parameterEstimates(fit, standardized = TRUE)
  rows <- estimates$op == "=~"
  knitr::kable(
    estimates[rows, c("lhs", "rhs", "est", "std.all")],
    col.names = c("Factor", "Indicator", "Estimate", "Std. loading"),
    digits = 3, row.names = FALSE
  )
}

5.1 Uncorrelated factors

The model fixes factor covariance at zero. The label a equates the two raw Attribution Bias loadings for identification with unit factor variance.

orthogonal_spec <- '
  anxiety =~ q01 + q03 + q04 + q05 + q08
  attribution =~ a*q06 + a*q07
  anxiety ~~ 0*attribution

5.2 Correlated factors

This model estimates the factor covariance and lets the computer-item loadings differ. With std.lv = TRUE, the covariance is also the correlation. A nonzero association with SPSS Anxiety helps identify the two computer-item loadings.

  anxiety =~ q01 + q03 + q04 + q05 + q08
  attribution =~ q06 + q07
  anxiety ~~ attribution

6 Second-order CFA

Overall Anxiety predicts the two first-order factors. Each first-order factor uses its first item as the marker. Both higher-order loadings are fixed to 1 for identification, and the overall factor variance is estimated. The first-order factors’ residual covariance is fixed to zero.

hierarchy_spec <- '
  anxiety =~ 1*q01 + q03 + q04 + q05 + q08
  attribution =~ 1*q06 + q07
  overall =~ 1*anxiety + 1*attribution
  overall ~~ overall
  anxiety ~~ 0*attribution
'
hierarchy <- cfa(hierarchy_spec, data = saq)

8 Practice questions and answers

  1. What is the difference between exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis?

    EFA explores possible factor structures. CFA tests whether a prespecified structure reproduces the observed covariances.

  2. What are the observed indicators and latent factors in the SAQ-8 example?

    The observed indicators are q01–q08. The seven-item models use SPSS Anxiety (q01, q03–q05, q08) and Attribution Bias (q06–q07). The hierarchical model adds overall Anxiety.

  3. What do factor loadings represent?

    A loading is the expected item-score change per unit of the factor. Standardized loadings use standard-deviation units. The .861 loading for q07 shows a stronger association with Attribution Bias than the .596 loading for q06.

  4. What do residual variances represent?

    Residual variance is the item variance left unexplained by its factor, including item-specific variation and measurement error. For q07, about 26% remains unexplained in the correlated model.

  5. How many unique variances and covariances are available for a model with three observed indicators?

    There are six unique entries, with three variances and three covariances, from p(p+1)/2=3(4)/2=6p(p+1)/2 = 3(4)/2 = 6.

  6. What is the difference between a fixed parameter and a free parameter?

    A fixed parameter is set in the model, such as the q03 marker loading of 1. A free parameter, such as the marker model’s factor variance, is estimated.

  7. Why is a model with negative degrees of freedom under-identified?

    Negative degrees of freedom mean there are more independent free parameters than observed moments, so the parameters cannot be uniquely estimated.

  8. Why does fixing one loading and setting residual covariances to zero help identify a model?

    A fixed marker loading sets the factor’s unit. Setting residual covariances to zero reduces the unknowns and attributes shared item variation to the factor. This leaves six free parameters for six observed moments in the three-item model.

  9. What is the difference between a just-identified model and an over-identified model?

    A just-identified model has zero degrees of freedom and can reproduce the observed moments exactly. An over-identified model has positive degrees of freedom, allowing a global fit test.

  10. How should chi-square, CFI, TLI, and RMSEA be interpreted?

    Chi-square tests exact fit, with a small p-value indicating misfit. Higher CFI and TLI indicate better fit relative to an independence baseline, and TLI accounts for model complexity. Lower RMSEA indicates less approximate misfit per degree of freedom. The eight-item model shows misfit across these measures.

  11. What is the difference between correlated and uncorrelated factors?

    A correlated model estimates the covariance between factors. An uncorrelated model fixes it at zero, implying zero cross-factor item covariance when cross-loadings and residual covariances are also zero.

  12. What does a second-order factor represent?

    A second-order factor explains covariance among first-order factors. Here overall Anxiety accounts for the association between SPSS Anxiety and Attribution Bias.

  13. Which model appears most theoretically and statistically appropriate based on the results?

    I would use the correlated seven-item model. Its fit is good (CFI = .986, TLI = .977, RMSEA = .040), and its two dimensions have clear interpretations. The hierarchy gives the same fit.

  14. What additional information would you want before making a final decision about the measurement model?

    I would check item wording and coding, the effect of omitting q02, subscale reliability and validity, and fit with an ordinal estimator. I would also replicate the model in a new sample and look for more indicators of Attribution Bias.

9 Summary of important lessons

  • Check identification before testing fit.
  • Read loadings, residual variances, and fit indices together.
  • Compare models on the same items. Equal fit can leave competing explanations unresolved.
